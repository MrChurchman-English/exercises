The rise of the weekend athlete

Why the fastest-growing group in endurance sport is nothing like the one you picture

Twenty years ago, few people would have that jogging around a local park at eight o'clock on a Saturday morning would become a national habit. to popular belief, the typical competitor at a mass-participation running event today is not a lean twenty-year-old in expensive kit, but a middle-aged parent with a full-time job. Amateur endurance sport has become a genuine growth over the past decade, and the numbers show no sign of falling.

For most people, it starts with a burning to prove something to themselves. What almost nobody admits is that running rarely naturally at first. Beginners are nowhere as fit as they imagine, and the initial few weeks can a considerable toll on both the body and the ego.

The advice from sports scientists is usually less dramatic than the advice found online. Nobody needs to out entire food groups, and nobody needs to train twice a day. What matters is consistency: it typically takes six weeks or more before the benefits of regular exercise begin to in, and most people abandon the habit long before that point. The old belief that more miles automatically produce better results has now been disproved, and much of the current thinking on recovery from research carried out in the 1990s.

The real challenge, then, is to strike a between training seriously and living a normal life. Runners who try to keep with rivals half their age tend to pick up injuries, while those who accept their limits improve steadily for years. Dr Elena Marsh, a physiologist who has much of her career to the study of ageing athletes, notes that participation always falls in January, when temperatures and enthusiasm fades with them.

Those who survive the winter, however, describe the experience in surprisingly emotional terms. Crossing a finish line, for many, is less about the time on the clock than about the chance to an ambition they have carried quietly for most of their adult lives.
